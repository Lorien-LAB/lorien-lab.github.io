## Public content model

### Core idea

A behavioral answer is credible when its conclusion is supported by observable evidence. The purpose is not to guess a preferred personality or memorize a universal response. It is to explain real choices and behavior in a way that lets the interviewer evaluate relevance, judgment, collaboration, and learning.

### Four-part answer structure

Use one reusable structure:

1. **Claim** — answer the question directly.
2. **Evidence** — give a specific real example, behavior, or result.
3. **Relevance** — connect the evidence to the role, team, or decision.
4. **Reflection** — explain what changed, what was learned, or what would be done differently.

The page must distinguish this from a memorized script. Structure organizes evidence; it does not replace authenticity.

### Evidence quality

Teach candidates to prefer:

- a bounded real event over a general trait label;
- their own contribution over vague team credit;
- observable action and outcome over self-praise;
- honest uncertainty or limitation over invented expertise;
- current organization research over generic compliments;
- reflection and improvement over defensive justification.

### Prompt families

Organize the 17 public prompts into five families:

1. motivation and role/company fit;
2. CV, research, and technical explanation;
3. strengths, weaknesses, self-awareness, and achievements;
4. conflict, deadlines, teamwork, leadership, and execution;
5. adaptability, tools, and transferable skills.

### Answer preparation workflow

1. inventory real examples;
2. label each example by capability demonstrated;
3. identify facts, contribution, outcome, and reflection;
4. adapt detail to the audience;
5. connect the example to the target role without pretending certainty;
6. rehearse retrieval and structure, not exact wording;
7. update the story when evidence or role understanding changes.

### Authenticity and integrity boundary

The page explicitly rejects:

- invented stories;
- borrowed accomplishments;
- scripted claims with no evidence;
- manipulating or flattering difficult colleagues;
- pretending every weakness is a disguised strength;
- giving a mandatory “yes” to culture/tool questions;
- claiming interest based on ownership, food, prestige, or compensation;
- repeating a book’s preferred answer.

### Practice Prompts

Publish exactly 17 independently worded prompts, each with no supplied personal answer:

1. Why are you pursuing quantitative work and this role now?
2. Choose one CV item: what did you contribute, why is it relevant, and what did you learn?
3. Why are you leaving or changing your previous direction?
4. Describe a difficult collaboration and the concrete actions you took.
5. What genuine weakness are you improving, and what evidence shows progress?
6. Give an example of meeting an important deadline under constraints.
7. What direction do you want your work to take over the next several years, and why does this role fit?
8. Explain your research first to a non-specialist and then to a technical expert.
9. What useful qualities do you bring beyond technical ability, and how have you demonstrated them?
10. What would close collaborators say about your working style, and what examples support that view?
11. What achievement best demonstrates unusual initiative or impact?
12. What have you studied or built that demonstrates genuine interest in finance or quantitative work?
13. What recent development at this organization is relevant to the role, and why?
14. Why does this organization or team fit your goals better than plausible alternatives?
15. In what situations do you work best independently, and when does collaboration improve the result?
16. Describe a time you led others toward a measurable outcome.
17. How would you evaluate and adapt to an unfamiliar internal language or tool while protecting transferable skills?

The page may show evaluation questions for each prompt, but must not generate fictional user-specific answers.

### Common mistakes

Cover unsupported traits, excessive negativity about prior work, generic company praise, answers that omit personal contribution, examples with no result, false certainty, irrelevant anecdotes, and confusing rehearsal with memorization.

### Interview Checks

Include at least six self-tests covering evidence versus labels, personal contribution, audience adaptation, honest weakness, company-specific research, and authenticity under pressure.
