## Problem

Assume a family has exactly two children. Each child's sex is independently boy or girl with probability `1/2`.

Compare the probability that both children are boys under the following information protocols:

1. You are told only that **at least one child is a boy**.
2. You are told that the **older child is a boy**.
3. One of the two children is selected uniformly at random; you observe that selected child and see a boy.
4. You are told that the family has a boy with a particular name. Is there a universal numerical answer without further assumptions?

## Think Before Revealing

Do not start from a memorized answer such as `1/3` or `1/2`. First write down exactly what random experiment generated the information.

## Solution

Before any information is observed, the four ordered family states

`BB, BG, GB, GG`

have probability `1/4` each.

The key is that the **observation mechanism** changes across the protocols.

### Protocol 1: at least one child is a boy

The conditioning event removes only `GG`. The remaining equally likely states are

`BB, BG, GB`.

Exactly one of the three is `BB`, so

`P(BB | at least one B)=1/3`.

### Protocol 2: the older child is a boy

The compatible states are only

`BB, BG`.

They had equal prior probability, hence

`P(BB | older child is B)=1/2`.

The difference from Protocol 1 is not a paradox: the conditioning event is different.

### Protocol 3: a uniformly selected child is observed to be a boy

Now augment the experiment with the selected child. Let `O` be the event that the uniformly selected child is observed to be a boy.

For a `BB` family,

`P(O | BB)=1`.

For `BG` or `GB`,

`P(O | BG)=P(O | GB)=1/2`.

For `GG`,

`P(O | GG)=0`.

Bayes' rule gives

`P(BB | O) = [(1)(1/4)] / [(1)(1/4)+(1/2)(1/4)+(1/2)(1/4)]`.

The denominator is `1/2`, so

`P(BB | O)=1/2`.

Equivalently, among all boy-observations generated by uniformly selecting one child, a two-boy family is twice as likely to produce the observation as a mixed family.

### Protocol 4: a named child

There is **not enough information for a universal numerical answer** merely from the sentence “the family has a boy with a particular name.” The answer depends on the information generation protocol.

A complete model may need to specify, for example:

- the frequency of that name among boys;
- whether siblings' names are independent or constrained to be distinct;
- whether the family was sampled first and then the fact was reported, or families were selected because they satisfied the named-child property;
- whether an interviewer chose which true fact to reveal and by what rule.

Different assumptions can reweight `BB`, `BG`, and `GB` differently. Therefore the named-child variant is **model-dependent** until those assumptions are stated.

## Why This Problem Matters

Two-child puzzles are really questions about conditioning on information. Their value is not the number `1/3`; it is recognizing that probability depends on the experiment that produced the statement.

The same issue appears in survivorship bias, case-control sampling, disclosure rules, selective reporting, diagnostic screening, and financial data sets conditioned on being observed.

## Common Mistakes

- Giving `1/3` to every sentence containing “one child is a boy.”
- Treating “at least one is a boy” as equivalent to “the older one is a boy.”
- Ignoring the random selection of a child in Protocol 3.
- Assuming a named-child variant has a canonical numerical answer without specifying naming and reporting assumptions.
- Counting compatible states equally when the observation mechanism makes them unequally likely.

## Extensions & Variants

A useful general rule is: when information is generated by observing one feature from a hidden state, augment the sample space with the observation process. Then compute

`P(hidden state | observation)`

rather than conditioning on an informal paraphrase of the observation.

This method resolves many apparent probability paradoxes without special tricks.
